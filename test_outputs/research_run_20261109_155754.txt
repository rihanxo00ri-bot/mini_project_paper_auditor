## Direct answer

The paper uses a **simulation-plus-measurement antenna design method** to build a four-port MIMO antenna from **concentric square-ring microstrip patches**, with **polarization and angle diversity** for decoupling. Mutual coupling is reduced by **etching two complementary split-ring resonators (CSRRs) into the ground plane** at the points of strong coupling. The design is simulated in CST Microwave Studio and validated with a fabricated prototype. Diversity is assessed with the **envelope correlation coefficient computed from far-field radiation patterns (Eq. 1)**.

Note: the paper's text was extracted with page markers. Page numbers below refer to the journal pages (PDF pages 1–4).

## Method components, with evidence

**1. Antenna structure: concentric square-ring patches (p. 1–2)**
- "A novel four port multiple-input-multiple-output (MIMO) antenna system is designed using concentric square ring patch antennas" (p. 1, Abstract).
- The design "uses concentric ring patches resonating in different transverse magnetic modes" (p. 1, Sec. II). It started from a single ring resonating in two fundamental orthogonal modes for P1 and P2, to achieve compactness (p. 1).
- Substrate: FR4, εr = 4.4, tan δ = 0.02, thickness 1.6 mm (p. 1). Simulations were run in CST Microwave Studio (p. 1).
- Dimensions are listed on p. 2 (W1 = 60 mm, W2 = 58 mm, W4 = 27.4 mm, W5 = 6.6 mm, etc.).
- Feed positions were chosen "so as to excite higher order orthogonal modes with high isolation between them" (p. 2).

**2. Diversity strategy: polarization and angle diversity (p. 1, p. 3)**
- The intro states the aim is to reduce size "by making use of polarization diversity and angle diversity" (p. 1).
- Ports P1/P4 are X-polarized and P2/P3 are Y-polarized (p. 2).
- Mode identification from current distributions (p. 3): P1 and P2 excite TM10 and TM01 (half-wavelength paths); P3 and P4 excite TM21 and TM12.
- Far-field patterns on p. 3 show P1/P2 have perpendicular polarization planes, and the higher-order modes have different pattern shapes, which the paper says "guarantees angle diversity."

**3. Bandwidth enhancement by slot loading (p. 2)**
- Half-wavelength slots are etched on each ring arm ("reactive loading"). Bandwidth rises from 50 MHz to 80 MHz, a 60% gain (p. 2, Fig. 4). The slits also reduce coupling between orthogonal higher-order ring modes (p. 2).
- The text also reports a 2:1 VSWR bandwidth of 75 MHz for the ring antenna, about 40% improvement (p. 2). This number differs from the 80 MHz quoted for the slotted version, so the paper's bandwidth figures should be read in their own context.

**4. Isolation method: CSRR on the ground plane (p. 2–3)**
- Two mutual-coupling sources are identified: surface-wave coupling through the substrate and current on the shared ground (p. 2, Sec. III).
- Two CSRRs are etched on the ground at the antenna corners between the concentric rings, tilted at 45°, at the points where coupling currents are strongest (p. 2). CSRR dimensions come from "the design formula in [9]" (p. 2). The paper does not reproduce that formula.
- Mechanism as stated by the authors (p. 2): the CSRR interacts with the magnetic field along Z, disturbing the ground currents. The ground current on the adjacent ring reverses polarity by about 180°, so the vertically polarized waves of P1 and P4 are out of phase, giving least coupling. The CSRR is said to shift the band-stop at the frequency of interest by changing equivalent L and C.
- Result: isolation improves by 6.5 dB (p. 3, Fig. 6); the final isolation is at least −22 dB (p. 4, Conclusion). The paper says the resonant-frequency shift is very small with CSRR (p. 3).

**5. Simulation and measurement workflow (p. 3)**
- Simulated S-parameters without and with CSRR (Figs. 7 and 8, p. 3).
- Prototype fabricated and measured with an R&S ZVB20 PNA vector network analyzer (p. 3, Fig. 9). The paper states "reasonable agreement" between simulated and measured results (p. 3).
- Far-field patterns measured in an anechoic chamber, exciting one port at a time with the other three terminated in matched loads (p. 3, Fig. 10).
- Reported gains: 4 dBi (inner ring modes) and 3.4 dBi (outer ring modes). Efficiencies: 84.6% (inner) and 72.5% (outer) (p. 3).

**6. MIMO performance: envelope correlation from radiation patterns (p. 4)**
- The paper states that correlation "can be computed from S parameters or from far field radiation patterns," and that the S-parameter approach "does not provide exact results," so the authors prefer the pattern-based method using Eq. (1) (p. 4).
- The propagation model is an outdoor urban environment, described as Gaussian in elevation and uniform in azimuth, citing [10] (p. 4).
- XPR = PV/PH is the cross-polarization ratio. Eθi and EΦi are the complex far-field components when only port i is excited with the other ports terminated in 50 Ω (p. 4). Pθ and PΦ are the incident-power distributions for the two polarizations.
- Result: correlation coefficients stay below 0.4 in the operating band (p. 4, Fig. 11).

## Interpretation (my reading, not stated by the paper)

The overall method is a **design-by-simulation loop with physical mode analysis**. The concentric rings give compactness, the feed positions and slot loading tune the modes and bandwidth, and the CSRR addresses the remaining coupling. Mode identification from surface-current plots (Fig. 6) is used to justify the diversity claims. Envelope correlation is then computed from measured or simulated patterns. The CSRR method is the paper's central isolation technique, and it is described qualitatively in the text.

- The values of PV, PH, and the angular distribution parameters used in Eq. (1) are not given in the text.

- The paper does not state how the 2:1 VSWR bandwidth figure (75 MHz) relates to the 80 MHz figure given for the slotted design (p. 2). The text is ambiguous on this point.
